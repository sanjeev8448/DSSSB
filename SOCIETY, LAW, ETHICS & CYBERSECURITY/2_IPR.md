Intellectual Property Rights (IPR) —
## 1. Copyright

Definition: Copyright is a legal right that protects original literary, artistic, musical, dramatic, and certain other creative works, including computer programs.

In computer science, copyright can protect source code, software documentation, website content, photographs, videos, and original graphics.

### Key features

- Protects the expression of an idea, not the underlying idea itself.
    
- Generally arises automatically when an eligible original work is created and fixed in a form, subject to applicable law.
    
- Gives the copyright owner certain exclusive rights, such as reproduction and distribution, subject to legal exceptions.
    
- Does not mean that every use of a copyrighted work is prohibited; exceptions and licences may permit particular uses.
    

### Examples

- Copying another developer's original source code and distributing it without permission may infringe copyright.
    
- Reproducing a textbook chapter on a website without authorisation may violate copyright.
    
- Writing your own program to solve the same problem is not automatically copyright infringement simply because another program solves it too.
    

Exam point: Copyright protects the expression of an idea, not the idea, method, or concept in the abstract.

## 2. Patent

Definition: A patent is a legal right granted for an invention that satisfies the applicable legal requirements. It generally allows the patent holder to prevent others from commercially exploiting the patented invention without permission for a limited period.

### Key features

- Protects qualifying inventions, not merely creative expression.
    
- The invention must satisfy requirements such as novelty, inventive step, and industrial applicability under the relevant law.
    
- The inventor or applicant must follow the applicable patent application and examination process.
    
- A patent is territorial: protection depends on the jurisdictions where rights are obtained.
    
- In many jurisdictions, the standard patent term is generally 20 years from the relevant filing date, subject to applicable law and conditions.
    

### Example

A company develops a new qualifying mechanical device and obtains a patent. Others generally cannot commercially make, use, or sell the patented invention within the scope of the patent without permission, subject to applicable exceptions.

Important for computer science: Software-related inventions are treated differently across jurisdictions. In India, a computer program per se is excluded from patentability under Section 3(k) of the Patents Act, 1970. Do not assume that every software program or algorithm can be patented.

## 3. Trademark

Definition: A trademark is a sign that distinguishes the goods or services of one business from those of other businesses.

A trademark may include a brand name, logo, word, symbol, or other legally recognised identifying sign.

### Key features

- Identifies the commercial source of goods or services.
    
- Helps consumers distinguish one brand from another.
    
- Can protect a brand name or logo when the legal requirements are met.
    
- Trademark rights may be obtained through registration, and some jurisdictions also recognise certain unregistered rights.
    
- Protection can generally be renewed, subject to applicable law and requirements.
    

### Examples

- A software company's distinctive brand name.
    
- A company's logo on its website or product.
    
- A distinctive name used to market a mobile application.
    

### Copyright vs patent vs trademark

|Feature|Copyright|Patent|Trademark|
|---|---|---|---|
|Protects|Original creative expression|Qualifying invention|Brand identity|
|Example|Source code or a book|A qualifying technical invention|Brand name or logo|
|Main purpose|Protect creative works|Protect inventions|Distinguish commercial sources|
|How protection arises|Generally automatic for eligible works|Requires grant through the patent system|Depends on applicable law; registration is commonly used|
|Duration|Depends on work and applicable law|Generally limited; often 20 years from filing|Potentially renewable repeatedly|

Memory trick:

- Copyright → creative work
    
- Patent → invention
    
- Trademark → brand identity
    

## 4. IPR violation

Definition: IPR violation occurs when someone infringes another person's or organisation's intellectual property rights, contrary to the applicable law or licence terms.

### Common types of IPR violations

|Violation|Example|
|---|---|
|Copyright infringement|Copying and distributing protected software without authorisation|
|Patent infringement|Commercially exploiting a patented invention without permission, where infringement is established|
|Trademark infringement|Using a confusingly similar protected brand identifier in a way that infringes trademark rights|
|Software piracy|Unauthorised copying, distribution, or use of software|
|Plagiarism|Presenting another person's work or ideas as one's own; it may also involve copyright infringement, but the two concepts are not identical|
|Licence violation|Using open-source software contrary to the applicable licence conditions|

### How to avoid IPR violations

1. Use legally obtained software and content.
    
2. Read the licence before using, modifying, or distributing software.
    
3. Give appropriate attribution where required.
    
4. Obtain permission when the law or licence requires it.
    
5. Do not copy source code or proprietary materials without authorisation.
    
6. Check trademark and patent requirements before commercialising a product.
    

Important distinction: Plagiarism is primarily about attribution and presenting work as one's own. Copyright infringement concerns the violation of copyright rights. A case can involve both, but not every instance of plagiarism is copyright infringement.

## 5. Open source

Definition: Open-source software is software whose source code is made available under a licence that grants permissions to use, study, modify, and redistribute it, subject to the licence's conditions.

Open source does not mean that the software has no copyright or that every use is unrestricted.

### Key features

- Source code is available under an open-source licence.
    
- Users may have permission to inspect and modify the code.
    
- Redistribution is permitted under the licence's terms.
    
- Some licences require source-code disclosure when distributing certain modified or combined works.
    
- Other licences are more permissive and generally allow redistribution under fewer conditions.
    
- Open-source software can be free to download, but commercial support, hosting, or related services may cost money.

### Open-source licence types

|Licence|General characteristic|
|---|---|
|MIT|Permissive; allows broad reuse subject to its conditions|
|Apache 2.0|Permissive; includes express patent-licence provisions and other conditions|
|GNU GPL|Copyleft; distribution of covered derivative works generally triggers source-code and licence obligations|
|BSD|A family of generally permissive licences with licence-specific conditions|

Exam trap: Open-source software is not automatically in the public domain. Copyright may still apply, and users must follow the licence.

### Open source vs proprietary software

|Open-source software|Proprietary software|
|---|---|
|Source code is available under an open-source licence|Source code is generally controlled by the owner and not publicly licensed for unrestricted modification|
|Modification and redistribution may be permitted under licence terms|Modification and redistribution are usually restricted unless authorised|
|Examples: Linux, Firefox|Examples: Microsoft Windows, Adobe Photoshop|
