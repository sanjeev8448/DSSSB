## 1. Hacking

Definition: Hacking involves accessing, manipulating, or exploiting computer systems, networks, or accounts. Unauthorised hacking is illegal; authorised security testing is a legitimate cybersecurity activity.

### Types and examples

- Unauthorised access: Entering a system without permission.
    
- Website defacement: Changing a website's content without authorisation.
    
- Password attacks: Attempting to gain access to accounts using stolen or guessed credentials.
    
- Ethical hacking: Testing systems with proper authorisation to discover security weaknesses.
    

### Prevention

- Use strong, unique passwords.
    
- Enable multifactor authentication (MFA).
    
- Install security updates and patches.
    
- Configure firewalls and access controls.
    
- Conduct authorised security testing.
    

Exam trap: Not all hacking is criminal. Ethical hacking is performed with permission and within an agreed scope.

## 2. Phishing

Definition: Phishing is a social-engineering attack in which an attacker impersonates a trusted person or organisation to trick victims into revealing sensitive information or performing unsafe actions.

### How phishing works

1. An attacker sends a fake email, message, or link.
    
2. The message creates urgency or pretends to offer a benefit.
    
3. The victim clicks a link, opens an attachment, or enters credentials.
    
4. The attacker may steal information, install malware, or access an account.
    

### Example

A person receives an email that appears to come from their bank, warning that their account will be blocked unless they verify their password through a link. The link leads to a fraudulent website designed to steal credentials.

### Types of phishing

|Type|Target or method|
|---|---|
|Email phishing|Fake or deceptive emails sent to potential victims|
|Spear phishing|A targeted attack tailored to a particular person or organisation|
|Whaling|Targeting senior executives or other high-profile individuals|
|Smishing|Phishing through SMS or text messages|
|Vishing|Phishing through voice calls|

### Prevention

- Verify the sender and destination URL.
    
- Avoid clicking unexpected links or opening suspicious attachments.
    
- Never share passwords or OTPs in response to unsolicited messages.
    
- Access banking and official services through trusted apps or manually entered addresses.
    
- Report suspicious messages.
    

Exam trap: Phishing is a type of social engineering. Its main purpose is to deceive people, rather than necessarily exploiting a technical software vulnerability.

## 3. Eavesdropping

Definition: Eavesdropping is the unauthorised interception or monitoring of private communications to obtain information without the participants' knowledge or permission.

In cybersecurity, it may involve monitoring network traffic, listening to communications, or capturing data transmitted between devices.

### Example

An attacker monitors unsecured network traffic on a public Wi-Fi network to try to capture sensitive information.

### Types

- Network eavesdropping: Monitoring data travelling across a network.
    
- Wireless eavesdropping: Intercepting wireless communications.
    
- Passive eavesdropping: Observing or capturing communications without changing them.
    
- Active interception: Intercepting and potentially manipulating communications; this overlaps with attacks such as man-in-the-middle attacks.
    

### Prevention

- Use HTTPS and other appropriately configured encryption.
    
- Use secure Wi-Fi with strong security settings.
    
- Avoid transmitting sensitive information over insecure connections.
    
- Use a trusted VPN where appropriate, especially on untrusted networks.
    
- Keep devices and network equipment updated.
    

Exam trap: Eavesdropping focuses on intercepting or monitoring communications. It is different from phishing, which primarily relies on deception to trick a victim.

## 4. Ransomware

Definition: Ransomware is a type of malicious software (malware) that denies access to files or systems, commonly by encrypting data, and demands a ransom for restoring access or for another promised outcome.

Some ransomware attacks also steal data and threaten to publish it.

### How ransomware works

1. Infection

A malicious attachment, compromised website, or exploited vulnerability delivers malware.

2. Execution and impact

Files may be encrypted, systems disrupted, or data stolen.

3. Ransom demand

The attacker demands payment, often in exchange for a claimed decryption key or to prevent data exposure.

### Types of ransomware

- Crypto-ransomware: Encrypts files or data.
    
- Locker ransomware: Locks a device or prevents normal access to the system.
    
- Double-extortion ransomware: Steals data and encrypts it, then threatens to publish the stolen information.
    

### Prevention and response

- Maintain regular, tested backups, including offline or otherwise isolated copies.
    
- Keep operating systems and applications patched.
    
- Use endpoint protection and email filtering.
    
- Avoid suspicious attachments and links.
    
- Restrict user permissions and access to sensitive systems.
    
- If infected, isolate affected devices from the network, notify the security team, and follow the incident-response process.
    

Exam trap: Paying a ransom does not guarantee that files will be restored or stolen data deleted.

## 5. Fraud email

Definition: A fraud email is an email intended to deceive a recipient for financial gain, information theft, or another dishonest purpose.

Fraud emails often use impersonation, fake invoices, false prizes, payment demands, or fraudulent job offers.

### Common examples

- An email pretending to be from a bank requesting account credentials.
    
- A fake invoice asking an employee to transfer money.
    
- A message claiming that the recipient has won a prize and must pay a processing fee.
    
- A fake job offer requesting an advance payment.
    
- An email impersonating a manager and requesting an urgent transfer to a new bank account.
    

### Warning signs

- An unfamiliar or misleading sender address.
    
- Urgent threats or unusual pressure.
    
- Unexpected attachments or links.
    
- Requests for passwords, OTPs, or confidential information.
    
- Suspicious payment instructions or an unexpected change in bank details.
    
- Spelling errors or inconsistent branding, although professional-looking messages can also be fraudulent.
    

### What should you do?

1. Do not click links or open unexpected attachments.
    
2. Verify payment requests through a separate, trusted communication channel.
    
3. Do not reply with passwords, OTPs, or banking credentials.
    
4. Report the message to your organisation's IT/security team or email provider.
    
5. If you have already transferred money or disclosed credentials, contact your bank or service provider immediately and report the incident to the appropriate authorities.
    

Fraud email vs phishing: These concepts overlap. Phishing specifically seeks to trick victims into revealing information or taking an unsafe action. Fraud emails are a broader category that also includes deceptive payment requests and other email-based scams.

## 6. Identity theft

Definition: Identity theft occurs when someone obtains and misuses another person's personal or identifying information without authorisation, often to commit fraud or impersonate that person.

### Information that may be stolen

- Name, address, and date of birth.
    
- Aadhaar or PAN details.
    
- Bank account or payment information.
    
- Login credentials and passwords.
    
- Credit or debit card details.
    
- Identity documents and personal photographs.
    

### Examples

- Using another person's identity documents to open an account fraudulently.
    
- Taking over an email account using stolen credentials.
    
- Using stolen payment-card details to make unauthorised purchases.
    
- Impersonating a person online to deceive their contacts.
    

### How identity theft happens

- Phishing emails and fake websites.
    
- Data breaches and leaked databases.
    
- Malware and spyware.
    
- Social engineering and impersonation.
    
- Theft of documents or devices.
    
- Oversharing personal information publicly.
    

### Prevention

- Use unique passwords and multifactor authentication.
    
- Never share OTPs, PINs, or passwords.
    
- Review bank statements and account activity regularly.
    
- Share identity documents only when necessary through trusted channels.
    
- Secure devices and email accounts.
    
- Act quickly if your identity documents or credentials are compromised.
    

Exam trap: Identity theft is the misuse of another person's identity or personal information. It is not the same as simply stealing a physical device, although a stolen device can enable identity theft.

## 7. Important comparisons

|Cybercrime|Main objective or action|Key clue|
|---|---|---|
|Hacking|Unauthorised access or manipulation of systems|Breaking into a system|
|Phishing|Deceiving victims into unsafe actions|Fake login or verification link|
|Eavesdropping|Intercepting or monitoring communications|Capturing network traffic|
|Ransomware|Denying access and demanding a ransom|Encrypted files and ransom note|
|Fraud email|Deceiving recipients through email|Fake invoice, payment, prize, or job offer|
|Identity theft|Misusing someone else's identity|Impersonation or fraudulent use of personal details|

### Quick memory technique

- Hacking: Access
    
- Phishing: Deception
    
- Eavesdropping: Interception
    
- Ransomware: Lock or encrypt
    
- Fraud email: Email-based scam
    
- Identity theft: Impersonation
