## 1. Safe browsing

Definition: Safe browsing means using websites and online services carefully to reduce the risk of malware, scams, data theft, and privacy breaches.

### Essential safe-browsing practices

1. Check website addresses: Look for misspellings, suspicious domains, and unexpected redirects.
    
2. Check HTTPS: HTTPS encrypts the connection between your browser and the website, but it does not guarantee that the website itself is trustworthy.
    
3. Avoid suspicious links: Do not click unexpected links in emails, messages, or pop-ups.
    
4. Download from trusted sources: Avoid pirated software and unknown download websites.
    
5. Update your browser: Security updates fix known vulnerabilities.
    
6. Use secure networks: Avoid sensitive transactions over untrusted networks unless you have appropriate protection.
    
7. Review permissions: Grant websites access to your camera, microphone, location, or notifications only when needed.
    
8. Be careful with pop-ups: Fake virus warnings may try to trick you into installing malicious software or calling fraudulent support numbers.
    
9. Log out on shared devices: Especially when using email, banking, or other sensitive accounts.
    
10. Back up important data: Backups help recover information after device failure or certain cyberattacks.
    

### Important browser security terms

|Term|Meaning|
|---|---|
|HTTPS|Encrypts data transmitted between the browser and website|
|Cookies|Small pieces of data websites store in a browser|
|Private/incognito mode|Usually prevents browsing history from being saved locally after the session; it does not make you anonymous online|
|Firewall|Monitors and filters network traffic according to security rules|
|Antivirus/anti-malware|Detects, blocks, or removes malicious software|

Exam trap: The padlock or HTTPS indicator does not prove that a website is legitimate. Scam websites can also use HTTPS.

## 2. Password safety

Definition: Password safety involves creating, storing, and using passwords securely to prevent unauthorised access to accounts and systems.

### Characteristics of a strong password

- Long and difficult to guess.
    
- Unique for each account.
    
- Not based on easily available personal information, such as a birthday or phone number.
    
- Not reused across important accounts.
    
- Stored using a reputable password manager when appropriate.
    

A long passphrase made of several unrelated words can be easier to remember and harder to guess than a short, predictable password.

### Good password practices

|Good practice|Why it matters|
|---|---|
|Use unique passwords|Prevents one compromised password from exposing multiple accounts|
|Use a password manager|Helps create and store strong passwords|
|Enable MFA|Adds another layer of authentication|
|Never share passwords or OTPs|Reduces the risk of account takeover|
|Change compromised passwords promptly|Limits continued unauthorised access|
|Avoid entering passwords on suspicious sites|Reduces phishing risk|

### What is multifactor authentication (MFA)?

MFA requires two or more different types of authentication factors.

- Something you know: Password or PIN.
    
- Something you have: Security key or authenticator device.
    
- Something you are: Fingerprint or facial recognition.
    

Example: Signing in with a password and then approving a request using an authenticator app.

Exam trap: A password and a PIN are generally both knowledge factors. Using both does not, by itself, make authentication multifactor.

### Authentication vs authorisation

- Authentication: Verifies who you are.
    
- Authorisation: Determines what you are allowed to access or do.
    

For example, logging in verifies your identity; permissions determine whether you can view or edit a particular file.

## 3. Identity protection

Definition: Identity protection refers to practices that prevent personal or identifying information from being stolen, exposed, or misused.

### Information to protect

- Full name, address, and date of birth.
    
- Aadhaar and PAN details.
    
- Bank and payment information.
    
- Passwords, PINs, and OTPs.
    
- Personal photographs and identity documents.
    
- Account recovery details.
    

### Common threats

- Phishing and fake websites.
    
- Identity theft and impersonation.
    
- Data breaches.
    
- SIM-swap fraud.
    
- Social engineering.
    
- Malicious applications that steal personal data.
    

### How to protect your identity

1. Share personal information only when necessary.
    
2. Never disclose OTPs, passwords, or PINs to callers or message senders.
    
3. Verify requests for identity documents through trusted channels.
    
4. Enable MFA on important accounts.
    
5. Review bank transactions and account-login alerts.
    
6. Avoid oversharing personal details on social media.
    
7. Secure lost or stolen devices and report suspected fraud quickly.
    

Example: A caller claims to be from a bank and requests an OTP to prevent account suspension. Do not disclose it. Contact the bank using its official number or app.

Exam trap: An OTP is intended to verify or authorise a specific action. Sharing it with an unverified person can enable account takeover or fraudulent transactions.

## 4. Confidentiality

Definition: Confidentiality is the security principle that ensures information is accessible only to authorised people, systems, or processes.

It is one of the three components of the CIA triad in information security.

C — Confidentiality

Prevents unauthorised disclosure of information.

Example: Encrypting confidential student records.

I — Integrity

Protects information against unauthorised or improper alteration.

Example: Preventing unauthorised changes to examination marks.

A — Availability

Ensures information and services are accessible when needed by authorised users.

Example: Maintaining reliable access to a college examination portal.

### Methods of maintaining confidentiality

- Encryption: Converts readable data into a protected form that requires the appropriate key to recover.
    
- Access control: Restricts access according to user permissions.
    
- Authentication: Verifies a user's identity before granting access.
    
- Data minimisation: Collects and retains only necessary information.
    
- Secure disposal: Prevents sensitive information from being recovered from discarded media.
    

### Confidentiality vs privacy

|Confidentiality|Privacy|
|---|---|
|Focuses on preventing unauthorised disclosure|Focuses on appropriate collection, use, sharing, and control of personal information|
|Example: Restricting access to medical records|Example: Deciding how personal information may be collected and used|

Exam trap: Encryption primarily helps protect confidentiality. Hashing is generally used to verify data integrity or store password verifiers, rather than to recover the original data.

## 5. Cyberbullying

Definition: Cyberbullying is bullying or harassment carried out through digital devices and online platforms. It may involve repeated or targeted behaviour intended to intimidate, humiliate, threaten, or harm someone.

### Examples

- Sending repeated threatening or abusive messages.
    
- Posting humiliating content about a person.
    
- Spreading harmful rumours online.
    
- Sharing private photographs without permission.
    
- Creating fake profiles to harass or impersonate someone.
    
- Deliberately excluding or targeting someone in online groups.
    

### Effects of cyberbullying

- Emotional distress, anxiety, or fear.
    
- Damage to reputation.
    
- Social isolation.
    
- Difficulty concentrating on education or work.
    
- Potential safety and privacy risks.
    

### How to respond

1. Do not retaliate or escalate the situation.
    
2. Save relevant evidence, such as screenshots, dates, and message links.
    
3. Block the account where appropriate.
    
4. Report the content or account to the platform.
    
5. Seek help from a trusted adult, teacher, employer, or appropriate authority.
    
6. Report threats, stalking, or other potentially criminal conduct to the appropriate authorities.
    

Exam trap: Cyberbullying can occur through private messages as well as public posts. It does not require a public audience.

## 6. Cyber trolling

Definition: Cyber trolling is the act of posting provocative, inflammatory, misleading, or offensive content online to provoke reactions, disrupt discussions, or upset other users.

### Common forms

- Deliberately posting provocative comments.
    
- Making inflammatory statements to start arguments.
    
- Repeatedly derailing an online discussion.
    
- Posting deliberately misleading content to provoke people.
    
- Baiting users into emotional or hostile responses.
    

### How to deal with trolling

- Avoid feeding deliberate provocation.
    
- Respond with facts when a response is useful.
    
- Block or mute persistent trolls.
    
- Report threats, harassment, or platform-rule violations.
    
- Keep discussions focused on the topic.
    

### Cyberbullying vs cyber trolling

|Cyberbullying|Cyber trolling|
|---|---|
|Targets a person or group for bullying or harm|Often aims to provoke reactions or disrupt discussion|
|May involve threats, humiliation, or repeated harassment|May involve inflammatory, misleading, or provocative posts|
|Can have serious personal and psychological effects|Can disrupt communities and may overlap with harassment|
|Example: Repeatedly threatening a classmate online|Example: Posting deliberately inflammatory comments to provoke an argument|

Important: The two can overlap. Trolling that targets and repeatedly harasses a person can also constitute cyberbullying.
